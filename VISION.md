# Vision Document: Employee Handbook as an Instruction Booklet

## Core Idea

We want to create a proof-of-concept employee handbook that lives on the web, but does not feel like a conventional website.

The goal is to explore what an employee handbook could become if it were treated less like a policy portal and more like a designed artefact: something closer to an old video game instruction booklet, a field guide, or a manual discovered inside a world.

This should feel like a handbook first and a website second.

The web is the medium. The handbook is the experience.

## Creative Direction

The inspiration is the feeling of classic game manuals and instruction booklets: compact, visual, curious, slightly mysterious, and deeply intentional.

A useful reference point is the instruction booklet aesthetic seen in *Tunic*: not to copy it literally, but to draw from the sense that the booklet itself is part of the experience. It feels tactile, illustrated, layered, and worth examining. It invites the reader to slow down, look closely, and discover how things work.

That is the emotional territory we want to explore.

The employee should not feel like they are browsing a standard HR website. They should feel like they are opening a carefully made guide to the organization.

## What This Is Not

This should not become a generic Tailwind-style marketing site.

It should not feel like a startup landing page, a SaaS dashboard, a documentation wiki, a slide deck, or a normal employee intranet.

It should not rely on generic cards, generic icon grids, generic gradients, generic rounded panels, or generic corporate web patterns unless they have been deliberately transformed into the language of a handbook.

It should not feel like content was placed into a template.

The visual form should carry the concept.

## What It Should Feel Like

The experience should feel authored.

It should have the sensibility of an object that someone designed with care: a manual, booklet, guide, map, or set of pages that belong to a specific world.

It may feel tactile, illustrated, printed, annotated, worn, symbolic, playful, or mysterious. It may use pages, spreads, margins, stamps, diagrams, sketches, symbols, maps, notes, page numbers, or other booklet-like devices.

These are not mandatory components. They are signals of the desired creative space.

The important thing is that the final demo should clearly reject the default visual language of modern corporate web design.

## Relationship to the Employee Handbook

This is still an employee handbook.

It should remain useful, readable, and grounded in real employee needs. The concept should make the handbook more engaging and memorable, not less clear.

The format should help communicate culture, expectations, orientation, resources, and ways of working. It should make the reader feel like they are being introduced to how the organization works, not simply being handed a set of rules.

The best version of this concept balances usefulness with atmosphere.

## Design Freedom

The implementation team should have room to interpret the idea.

They do not need to copy any specific game, manual, layout, illustration style, or interaction model. They should instead capture the broader qualities of an instruction booklet: intentional sequencing, visual explanation, compact guidance, diagrams, texture, discovery, and a sense of place.

The team should feel free to invent a visual system that serves the organization rather than reproducing a reference too directly.

The proof of concept should demonstrate taste, imagination, and restraint.

## Non-Negotiables

The demo must feel like a handbook, manual, or booklet rather than a normal website.

It must be visually distinctive.

It must avoid generic corporate web design.

It must not feel like a standard Tailwind template with handbook content added afterward.

It must show that the web experience has been designed around the handbook concept from the beginning.

It must be usable enough that the concept feels viable, not merely decorative.

## Desired Outcome

The proof of concept should help people understand the possibility:

An employee handbook does not have to be a flat document or a searchable HR portal. It can be a designed experience. It can introduce an organization through mood, structure, illustration, rhythm, and discovery.

The goal is not to finalize the entire handbook system. The goal is to prove that this direction has power.

A successful demo should make someone think:

“This feels like an employee handbook I would actually want to open.”

And just as importantly:

“This could not have been achieved by dropping content into a generic website template.”
