# Handbook POC — Fictional Organization

This document defines the fictional organization that the employee handbook proof-of-concept describes. Every proper noun, role reference, tool name, and structural detail in the handbook content traces back to this document. No real organization, product, or person is referenced.

## Purpose

The handbook must read as a real guide to a real organization. A reader should be able to form an opinion of the organization from the content alone — its values, its rhythms, its character. This document keeps those details consistent across chapters and prevents accidental drift into generic corporate text.

## The Organization

**Name:** None in the content. The handbook refers to "the organization" or "this organization." No brand name, logo, or trademark appears. The organization is defined by how it works, not by what it calls itself.

**Nature:** A small product company — roughly 40 people — that designs, builds, and supports software products. It has been operating for several years and has developed a deliberate culture around how work happens.

**What it makes:** Software products, primarily web applications. The Product domain owns decisions about what to build and for whom. Craft owns how it is built. People owns how the team grows and supports each other.

## Structure

### Three Domains

The organization is understood through three domains, not through a traditional functional org chart:

| Domain | Focus | What it answers |
| ------ | ----- | --------------- |
| **Product** | What we build and why | Who are we building for? What problem are we solving? What should we do next? |
| **Craft** | How we work | What standards do we hold? How do we improve? How do we build things well? |
| **People** | How we grow | How do we support each other? How do we hire, onboard, give feedback, and develop careers? |

Every person in the organization participates in all three domains. Someone primarily working in Product still contributes to Craft and People. The domains are lenses, not departments.

### Teams

Work happens in small, cross-functional teams — typically 3–5 people — formed around specific problems or products. Teams have a defined purpose and a clear scope. They decide how they work within the organization's shared standards.

### Roles referenced in the handbook

| Role | Description |
| ---- | ----------- |
| Domain steward | A person who holds context for a domain — not a manager, but someone who notices patterns, connects people, and helps the domain stay healthy. Each domain has 1–2 stewards. |
| Team lead | Rotates. Not a permanent title. The person who facilitates a team's rhythm for a given cycle. |
| Arrival guide | A person assigned to a new hire for their first two weeks. Answers questions, introduces people, and helps the newcomer find their way. Not a formal mentor — more like a field guide. |

## Tools & Platforms (fictional names)

Where the handbook references specific tools, it uses these fictional names. They are chosen to suggest their purpose without mimicking any real product name.

| Fictional name | Purpose | What it replaces in a real org |
| -------------- | ------- | ----------------------------- |
| The Signal | Primary asynchronous communication platform — threaded discussion channels | Slack, Discord, Teams |
| The Record | Long-form documentation and decision records | Notion, Confluence, Google Docs |
| The Forge | Source code hosting, code review, and CI/CD | GitHub, GitLab, Bitbucket |
| The Map | Task tracking and project visibility | Linear, Jira, Trello |
| The Workshop | Local development environment — containerized, one-command start | Docker Compose, Dev Containers |

## Communication Culture

The organization defaults to asynchronous, written communication. Decisions are made in public channels and documented where others can find them later. Meetings exist but are intentional — they have a clear purpose, a prepared agenda, and produce written notes.

The principle: a decision that is not written down did not happen.

## Values (expressed as observable behaviors)

The handbook describes values as field marks — behaviors you can observe, not abstractions you must interpret:

- **Work in the open.** Discussions happen in public channels. Documents are shared by default. Context is not hoarded.
- **Ask early, ask often.** Pretending to know is worse than asking. Questions are a sign of engagement, not incompetence.
- **Document what you learn.** If something was hard to figure out, write it down so the next person does not have to.
- **Craft over speed.** Deliberate, improving practice produces better results over time than rushing. Quality is the result of a thousand small decisions.
- **People over process.** A process is a tool, not a master. If a process is not serving the people using it, change it.

## Calendar Rhythm

The organization runs on a deliberately minimal meeting cadence:

| Cadence | What happens |
| ------- | ----------- |
| Monday morning | Team check-in — 15 minutes, written. What is everyone working on? What is blocked? |
| Wednesday | No-meeting day. Deep work. |
| Thursday mid-day | Domain sync — each domain holds open office hours. Drop in if you need something. |
| Friday afternoon | Demo and reflect — what shipped this week? What did we learn? 30 minutes, all welcome. |
| Every two weeks | Team retrospective — what is working? What should change? |

Everything else is ad-hoc and scheduled only when needed.

## Decision Log

- **2026-06-13:** Organization name intentionally excluded from handbook content. Rationale: the handbook proves the format works without brand identity. A real organization adopting this format would add its own name, voice, and specifics.
- **2026-06-13:** Three-domain model chosen over traditional departmental structure. Rationale: domains-as-lenses reinforces the field-guide metaphor (territories on a map) and avoids reproducing a generic org-chart page.
- **2026-06-13:** Tool names fictionalized. Rationale: referencing real products would date the handbook and tie the proof-of-concept to specific vendors. Fictional names with clear purposes let readers map to their own toolset.
- **2026-06-13:** Roles described by function, not title. Rationale: titles are organization-specific and change frequently. Functional descriptions (domain steward, arrival guide, team lead) communicate the role's purpose without implying hierarchy.
