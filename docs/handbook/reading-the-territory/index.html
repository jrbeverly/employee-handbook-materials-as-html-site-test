Reading the Territory

The Three Territories

The organization is arranged as three overlapping territories. Each is a lens for understanding how work happens. They are not departments. Nobody reports to a territory. They are ways of seeing.

Product — What We Build and Why

The Product territory is about understanding the landscape of needs and choosing where to focus. It answers the questions: Who are we building for? What problem are we solving? What should we work on next?

People working in the Product territory spend their time on problem discovery, user research, prioritization, and roadmapping. They talk to the people who use what we build. They bring that understanding back to the team.

The Product territory is not a department. Everyone in the organization participates in product thinking — noticing when something could be better, questioning whether the right problem is being solved, and advocating for the people we serve.

Key practices: writing problem statements before solutions, talking to users regularly, measuring outcomes not output, making prioritization explicit and visible.

Craft — How We Work

The Craft territory is about the standards we hold ourselves to and how we improve. It answers: How do we build things well? What does “good” look like? How do we get better?

Craft covers engineering standards, design practices, code review, testing, documentation, and the bar for quality. It is not about perfection. It is about deliberate, improving practice — the belief that how we build is as defining as what we build.

Everyone participates in Craft. A well-written bug report is a craft act. A thoughtful code review is a craft act. Documenting something you learned the hard way is a craft act.

Key practices: code review as conversation not gatekeeping, testing as documentation, documenting decisions with rationale, deliberate practice and skill-building.

People — How We Grow

The People territory is about hiring, onboarding, feedback, career development, and the culture we build together. It answers: How do we support each other? How do we grow? Who are we becoming?

People work is everyone’s work. Giving clear feedback, supporting a teammate who is stuck, interviewing a candidate with care, and noticing when someone needs help — these are People territory acts.

The organization believes that a healthy workplace grows its people as carefully as it grows its products. Career development is not a ladder. It is a landscape — with paths that branch, terrain that shifts, and discoveries that change your direction.

Key practices: regular feedback, mentorship and coaching, inclusive hiring processes, psychological safety as a daily practice, celebrating growth not just delivery.

Moving Between Territories

The territories overlap by design. A decision about what to build next (Product) is shaped by how well the team can build it (Craft) and who is available to do the work (People). A hiring decision (People) is shaped by what the organization needs to build (Product) and the standards it holds (Craft).

The paths between territories are the communication channels, decision processes, and collaborative practices that make the organization work. They are not formal reporting lines. They are the actual routes information travels.

When the paths are well-maintained — when people in different territories talk to each other regularly, when decisions are visible, when context is shared — the organization moves smoothly. When the paths are blocked, things get stuck.

Field mark: In a healthy organization, you hear people from different territories referencing each other’s work naturally. “I talked to someone in Product about this.” “Craft reviewed the approach.” “People raised a concern about the timeline.” When you stop hearing these cross-references, the paths are closing.

How to Use the Map

The organizational map on the preceding page shows the three territories as overlapping regions, connected by paths. It is not precise. It is not to scale. It is a way of thinking — a mental model you carry, not a blueprint you consult.

To orient yourself: Find the territory where you spend most of your time. That is your home terrain. Learn its features first.

To understand a decision: Trace it through all three territories. A decision that makes sense in Product may strain Craft or overlook People. The best decisions account for all three.

To find help: Each territory has stewards — people who hold context and connect others. If you need to understand something in a territory you do not know well, find its steward.

To notice trouble: When one territory dominates decisions consistently — when every conversation defaults to “what” (Product) without “how” (Craft) or “who” (People) — something is out of balance.

The map is not the territory. But a good map, like a good field guide, helps you find your way.
