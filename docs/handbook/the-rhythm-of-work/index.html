The Weekly Cadence

The working week has a deliberate shape. It is designed to create long stretches of uninterrupted time while keeping the organization synchronized enough to function.

Monday morning — team check-in. Fifteen minutes, written, not spoken. Each person posts in their team channel: What did I finish last week? What am I working on this week? What is blocked? No meeting required. The check-in is read, not presented.

Wednesday — deep work. No recurring meetings. No expected interruptions. The entire organization treats Wednesday as protected time for focused work. Schedule around it.

Thursday mid-day — domain office hours. Each domain steward holds an open hour. Drop in if you need something — a decision unblocked, a question answered, a connection made. Optional. Casual. But there if you need it.

Friday afternoon — demo and reflect. Thirty minutes, all welcome. What shipped this week? What did we learn? Not a status report — a celebration of work completed and an honest reflection on what could be better. Demos over slide decks, always.

Every two weeks — team retrospective. What is working? What should change? The retrospective is the mechanism for improving the way the team works. It belongs to the team, not to management.

The Shape of a Good Meeting

Meetings are not the default. When one is genuinely needed, it should earn its place:

  • Purpose before invitation. The invitation states what the meeting will decide or produce, not just what it will discuss.
  • An agenda written in advance. Attendees arrive having read it. The first ten minutes are not spent getting everyone on the same page.
  • Notes posted afterward. A summary, decisions made, and actions agreed go into the Record. If a meeting produced no written artifact, it might as well not have happened.
  • The right people, not all the people. Invite the people who need to be there. Send notes to everyone else.
  • A hard stop. Meetings end on time. If a decision is not reached, a new decision process begins — not a meeting that runs over.

If you are in a meeting and you do not know why you are there, it is acceptable to ask. “What decision am I here to help make?” is a respectful question. It often reveals that the meeting needed a better invitation, not that you are in the wrong room.

The Rhythm of Work

How Decisions Get Made

Not every decision needs a process. Most decisions are small, reversible, and belong to the person doing the work. Make them and move on.

For decisions that affect more than one person or are hard to reverse, the organization uses a lightweight framework:

Who Decides?

Every significant decision has a decider — one person accountable for making the call. The decider is not necessarily the most senior person. They are the person closest to the problem with enough context to make a good choice.

The decider’s job is not to decide alone. It is to gather input from the right people, weigh the options, make the call, and communicate it clearly.

Gathering Input

Before deciding, the decider consults people who have relevant expertise, who will be affected by the decision, and who have context the decider lacks. This is not consensus. It is input.

The decider writes a brief: Here is the decision we need to make. Here are the options I see. Here is what I am leaning toward and why. What am I missing?

Input happens in writing, in a public channel or a Record document. This way the reasoning is visible and the decision is traceable.

Making the Call

The decider makes the call and writes it down. A decision record includes:

  • What was decided
  • Why it was decided (the reasoning, not just the conclusion)
  • What alternatives were considered and why they were not chosen
  • Who provided input
  • When to revisit if the decision has a natural review point

This format — the decision record — is one of the most important documents the organization produces. It allows future readers to understand not just what was decided, but why. It is the difference between because I said so and here is the reasoning.

Disagree and Commit

Once a decision is made, everyone commits to it — even people who advocated for a different option. Disagreement happens before the decision. After the decision, the organization moves as one.

This is not blind obedience. It is a recognition that forward motion matters more than getting your preferred option. If the decision turns out to be wrong, the organization will learn that together and adjust. What it will not do is fragment into people quietly pursuing different directions.

Revisiting Decisions

A decision is not permanent. New information, changing circumstances, or observed results may call a past decision into question. When that happens, the same process applies: a decider, input gathering, a written record.

The bar for revisiting a decision should be higher than the bar for making it the first time — otherwise the organization spends all its time re-deciding. But the door is never closed.

A decision that is never revisited is not necessarily a good decision. It may be a decision nobody remembers the reason for. If you find yourself following a rule and you do not know why it exists, ask. The answer might be good. Or it might be “that made sense three years ago and nobody has questioned it since.” Both are useful to know.

How to Get Unstuck

Everyone gets stuck. The organization is designed to help you get unstuck quickly.

Step one: State that you are stuck. Say it in your team channel. Name the problem. Describe what you have tried. A clearly stated problem is often halfway to a solution.

Step two: Find the decider. If you are blocked on a decision, identify who can make it and ask them directly. Do not wait for the decision to find you. Decisions do not have legs.

Step three: Propose a path forward. Instead of “what should I do?”, try “here is what I think we should do, and here is why — does anyone object?” A proposal is easier to respond to than an open question.

Step four: Escalate with context. If the decider is unresponsive or the decision has wider implications, raise it in the relevant domain channel or office hours. Bring the context — what the decision is, what input you have gathered, and what you recommend.

The organization would rather you ask for help on Tuesday than struggle silently until Friday. Getting stuck is normal. Staying stuck alone is avoidable.
